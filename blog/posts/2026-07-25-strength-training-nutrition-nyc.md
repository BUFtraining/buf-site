Nutrition advice for NYC adults who strength train is drowning in noise. Seed oils will kill you. Fasting is the answer. Keto builds muscle. Carbs at night make you fat. Cortisol from your work stress is why you can't lose weight. Every week there's a new supplement, a new protocol, a new reason to add complexity to something that isn't actually that complicated.

After years of coaching nutrition for hundreds of BUF clients — from busy finance analysts eating dinner at their desks to Hell's Kitchen residents cooking for their families — the pattern is clear. Nutrition is 90% three things: **enough protein, appropriate total calories for your goal, and consistency over months rather than weeks.** Everything else is a rounding error.

This is a guide to what actually matters for nutrition if you're an NYC adult who strength trains. What to prioritize, what to ignore, how to eat out without wrecking your progress, and why tracking works better than any other approach we've seen for producing real long-term results.

## The three things that matter (and the thirty that don't)

Before we get into specifics, the framework:

**Things that matter, ranked by leverage:**

1. **Total daily calories** — aligned with your goal (deficit for fat loss, surplus for muscle gain, maintenance to hold current physique)
2. **Total daily protein** — enough to build and preserve muscle while you train
3. **Consistency** — sustained over months, not perfection for two weeks then falling apart

**Things that don't matter for most people:**

- Whether you eat carbs at night
- Whether you fast for 16 hours or eat breakfast at 7 AM
- Whether you eat organic
- Whether you eat "clean" or "dirty" foods (this dichotomy is largely nonsense)
- Whether you take fat burners, greens powders, or 90% of supplements
- Whether you have small frequent meals or a few big ones
- Whether you cycle your carbs or eat the same amount daily
- Whether specific foods are "inflammatory" for a healthy adult
- Whether you drink celery juice, apple cider vinegar, or any other trendy tonic
- Whether you eat "clean" carbs (rice, oats) vs "dirty" carbs (white bread) — calories and total intake dominate

You could get 95% of nutrition results from a strategy that says: *hit your protein target, eat roughly the right total amount of food, and stay consistent for six months.* Everything else is refinement — useful for the last 5%, but not where beginners should start.

## Protein: the one macro that matters most

For NYC adults who strength train, protein is the macro that moves the needle. It's what your body uses to build and preserve muscle, and it's the single most consistent factor in successful body composition changes.

**The number to hit:** 0.7 to 1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight per day. For most NYC adults, that lands somewhere between 130 and 200 grams daily.

- 150 lb woman: 105-150g protein per day
- 175 lb man: 125-175g protein per day
- 200 lb man: 140-200g protein per day

Most adults chronically under-eat protein without realizing it. A typical breakfast of avocado toast and coffee has around 10g. A grain bowl at lunch might have 20-25g. Dinner might have 30-40g if you order well. That's 60-75g total — barely half of what a 170-pound person needs to actually build muscle from their training.

**Practical protein sources for NYC eating patterns:**

- **Convenient and cheap:** Greek yogurt (17-22g per cup), cottage cheese (24g per cup), eggs (6g each), rotisserie chicken (30g per 3.5 oz), canned tuna (25g per can), deli turkey
- **Restaurant staples:** Grilled chicken (30-40g per breast), steak (40-50g per 6 oz), salmon (30g per 5 oz), Japanese sashimi (25-40g per order), Mediterranean chicken shawarma bowls (40-50g)
- **Supplements when needed:** A whey or plant-based protein shake adds 20-30g in seconds — genuinely useful for anyone struggling to hit their target from food alone

If you're not tracking, the practical shortcut is: **make sure every meal has a clear protein anchor.** Not just "some" protein — an actual portion of chicken, fish, red meat, eggs, or high-protein dairy that you can point to.

## Meal timing: what matters vs what doesn't

Meal timing is where the noise-to-signal ratio in fitness content is worst. Here's the honest breakdown:

**What actually matters:**
- Total daily intake (calories and protein)
- Protein distributed across the day (not all in one meal)
- Enough carbs and calories to fuel your training sessions

**What doesn't really matter for most people:**
- Exact protein timing around your workout (as long as you eat within 3-4 hours before or after, you're fine)
- Whether you eat every 2 hours or every 5 hours
- Whether you eat breakfast or fast until noon
- The specific "anabolic window" post-workout

For NYC adults who strength train, **three meals a day is the pattern that works best for most people.** Not because there's magic in three meals — but because three meals a day fits the reality of a working adult's schedule, delivers protein evenly across the day, and is sustainable long-term. Six small meals is a hassle. One giant dinner leaves you underfueled for training. Three anchored meals — breakfast, lunch, dinner — with an optional protein snack works for almost everyone.

If you're doing intermittent fasting because it fits your lifestyle (you're not hungry in the morning, you like the mental clarity, it simplifies your day), that's fine. If you're doing it because you think it's magic for fat loss, understand that it's not — it's just calorie restriction with fewer eating windows. Any results are from eating less, not from the timing.

## Eating out in NYC without wrecking your progress

This is where NYC adults have a unique problem that suburban clients don't face. You're not preparing 21 meals a week from your kitchen. You're eating out 8, 10, 15 times a week — team lunches, client dinners, dates, quick grabs when meetings run long, weekend brunches. The strategies that work in "eat home-cooked meals five nights a week" nutrition content don't apply.

Here's what actually works for eating out in NYC while making real progress:

**Order using the protein anchor framework.** Start every restaurant meal by identifying the protein source — chicken, fish, steak, eggs, tofu — and building the meal around it. Add a vegetable side. Include controlled starches if you have room in your calories. If a menu has no clear protein option, order two — grilled shrimp *and* the chicken.

**Cuisines that make this easy:**
- **Japanese/Sushi:** Sashimi is essentially pure protein. Add a seaweed salad. Skip the rice or eat half.
- **Mediterranean/Greek:** Grilled meats, olive oil-based sides, vegetables. Chicken kebabs, grilled octopus, greek salad — all great.
- **Korean BBQ:** Grilled meats plus vegetable sides. Easy to build protein-heavy meals.
- **Mexican (thoughtful ordering):** Fajitas, grilled fish tacos in lettuce wraps, chicken with rice and beans (portioned). Skip the chips.
- **Steakhouses:** Steak plus vegetables, ordered simply. Skip the bread basket.
- **Chinese (some options):** Steamed dishes with protein and vegetables, sauces on the side.

**Cuisines that require more care:**
- **Italian:** Pasta and bread dominate. Order a protein-focused main (grilled fish, veal, chicken parm without pasta) rather than a pasta dish. Or accept that pasta night is a treat and control portions.
- **Diners/breakfast spots:** Order eggs and add sides — skip pancakes, skip large stacks of toast.
- **Sandwich shops:** The bread is 30-40g of carbs before you count anything else. Order a bowl version if available.
- **Fried anything:** Not "banned" — just expensive calorically. Order occasionally, not weekly.

**About alcohol:** A glass of wine is 120-150 calories. Cocktails run 200-400. Craft beers are 200-300. Two drinks with dinner adds 300-800 empty calories to the meal. This is where most weight-loss efforts silently die. You don't have to abstain — you have to count it honestly and factor it into your week.

**The 80/20 principle actually works.** If 80% of your meals are protein-anchored with vegetables and controlled starches, you can afford the 20% that are pizza, ramen, or the tasting menu at a restaurant you've been wanting to try. What kills progress is when the ratio flips to 50/50 or worse.

## Tracking: why it works, and when it doesn't

The single most reliable predictor of nutrition success we see at BUF is whether the client tracks their food, at least for a period.

Not because tracking is magic — because tracking creates awareness. Most people who think they're eating "healthy" or "in a deficit" are actually eating 500-800 calories more than they estimate. They forget the coffee with milk. They underestimate portions. They don't count the handful of chips at the meeting. Tracking makes the invisible visible.

**How to track effectively:**

- Use an app: **MyFitnessPal, MacroFactor, or Cronometer** are all solid. MacroFactor has the best design if you want to pay; MyFitnessPal is free and has the largest food database.
- Track for at least 2-4 weeks initially to establish a baseline. You'll be surprised.
- Weigh your food when possible — visual estimates are wildly inaccurate for most people.
- Focus on protein first, total calories second. Don't stress about hitting exact carb and fat targets while you're learning.

**How long to track:**

Most successful BUF clients track intensively for 6-8 weeks. Then they transition to "loose tracking" — tracking one or two meals a day, or only tracking on days they're near their calorie ceiling. Eventually, most clients internalize portion sizes and food composition to the point that they don't need to track daily anymore. They'll come back to tracking for a few weeks during a specific goal push (leaning down for a vacation, resetting after a busy season), then transition back to intuitive eating.

**When tracking isn't right for you:**

Tracking isn't the right tool for everyone. If you have a history of disordered eating, if tracking causes you to obsess or restrict unhealthily, if it damages your mental relationship with food, don't use it. There are other paths — plate composition frameworks (half the plate vegetables, quarter protein, quarter starch), intuitive eating with clear protein anchors, or coaching-guided qualitative approaches. Talk to your doctor or a mental health professional if you have concerns about your relationship with food before starting any tracking regimen.

For the majority of healthy adults with normal relationships to food, tracking for 6-8 weeks then transitioning to internalized habits produces the best results we see.

## The common mistakes we see with NYC clients

After hundreds of BUF clients, the pattern of what goes wrong is remarkably consistent:

**Under-eating protein.** By far the most common mistake. Clients think they're eating "high protein" and are actually at 60-70g on a 170-lb body that needs 130-170g. Fixing this alone transforms results.

**Under-eating calories overall.** Especially common among women trying to lose fat. Cutting calories dramatically works for 4-6 weeks, then metabolism adapts, energy crashes, sleep suffers, training quality collapses, and the diet ends badly. Sustainable fat loss uses a modest deficit (300-500 calories below maintenance), not a dramatic one.

**All-or-nothing thinking.** One bad meal turns into a bad day. A bad day turns into "starting fresh Monday." Monday never comes. The mental frame is the problem, not the food.

**Weekend catastrophes.** Weekday discipline is undone by Saturday-Sunday behavior. If Saturday brunch (2000 calories) and Sunday dinner (1500 calories) are eaten without a plan, you've canceled Monday-Friday's deficit in two meals.

**Chasing the latest supplement.** Fat burners, adaptogens, greens powders — all money spent for effects that are minimal to non-existent. Money is better spent on real food or coaching.

**Restrictive diets that can't be sustained.** Keto, paleo, Whole30, carnivore — all can produce short-term results. But if you can't imagine yourself eating this way three years from now, it's not going to work three years from now. Sustainable > extreme.

**Not eating enough around training.** Under-eating on training days kills your session quality. If you're training hard, your body needs fuel. Eat before your session, eat after your session, and stop trying to train fasted unless you have a specific reason.

## What good progress actually looks like

Realistic expectations save you from quitting when your progress looks "slow" compared to Instagram promises.

**Fat loss (with strength training):**
- Sustainable rate: 0.5-1 lb per week
- Faster than 1-1.5 lb per week starts costing you muscle
- 5-10 lbs in 3 months is meaningful progress
- 15-25 lbs in 6 months is excellent progress

**Muscle gain:**
- Beginners: 0.25-0.5 lb of muscle per week for the first 6-12 months (impressive)
- Intermediates: 0.1-0.25 lb per week
- Advanced lifters: measured in single pounds per year
- Anyone claiming faster than this either has unusual genetics, uses PEDs, or is measuring water/glycogen not muscle

**Body composition:**
- Scale weight often stays flat while you lose fat and gain muscle
- Photos, measurements, and how clothes fit are more reliable than the scale
- Sleep quality, energy, and mood improve before visible changes

**Timeline reality:**
- 4-6 weeks: you feel different (better energy, clothes fit slightly different, strength up)
- 3 months: friends and family start noticing
- 6-12 months: significant visible transformation
- 2-3 years: the kind of change you'd see in a real "before/after"

The people who transform their bodies in NYC aren't doing it in 8 weeks. They're doing it consistently over years, using approaches they can maintain forever.
